Why Use Honeypots for Threat Detection ?

      

    

      

        
        
Why Use Honeypots for Threat Detection and Cyber Threat Intelligence ?

      

      

        

          
Security tools can only detect what they already know, making it necessary to observe and collect new attack techniques and malware used by cyber attackers.
Understanding cyber attackers requires observing their behavior, objectives, and methods during their activities.
A honeypot is a deliberately exposed system designed to attract attackers and record every interaction.

        

        
      

      
References: NIST SP 800-94 – Guide to Intrusion Detection and Prevention Systems (IDPS), TechTarget – What is a Honeypot ?.

      
    

      

        
        
Honeypots vs. Honeytokens: What's the Difference ?

      

      

        
        

          
Sometimes, defenders need to know what an attacker accesses, what they are looking for, or even their position within the information system.
A honeytoken is a fake digital asset, such as a file, credential, API key, or database record, designed to trigger an alert whenever it is accessed or used.
Unlike honeypots, which simulate vulnerable systems to observe attacker interactions, honeytokens are passive decoys that reveal unauthorized access to specific resources.

        

      

      
References: Honeyfiles: Deceptive Files for Intrusion Detection – IEEE Workshop on Information Assurance 2004.

      
    

      

        
        
What Can We Learn from Honeypot Data ?

      

      

        

          
Honeypots collect detailed information about attacker activity, providing insights into:
    
            
  • Attack trends and statistics: Measure attack volumes, sources, targeted services, and changes over time.
  • Indicators of Compromise (IOCs): Collect malicious IP addresses, domains, files, hashes, and other artifacts.
  • Attacker tools and techniques: Identify the tools used by attackers and analyze their Tactics, Techniques, and Procedures (TTPs).
  • Malware campaigns: Identify and understand malware campaigns targeting exposed systems.
  • Lateral movement: Detect attempts to access additional systems after initial compromise.
    • 
          

        

        
      

      
References: Honey Pot (computing) – IEEE Technology Navigator, Statistics - ProjectHoneyPot.

      
    

      

        
        
Who Uses Honeypots and Why ?

      

      

        
        

          
Universities and research laboratories use honeypots to study attack trends, global threat activity, attacker behaviors, and geographic distribution.
Security companies use honeypots to discover new malware campaigns, emerging exploits, attacker infrastructure, and new threats.
Security teams mainly use honeytokens and deception techniques to detect unauthorized access and generate high-confidence alerts.

        

      

      
References: A Practical Honeypot-Based Threat Intelligence Framework for Cyber Defence in the Cloud.

      
    

      

        
      

      

        
How Honeypots and Honeytokens Work for Threat Detection and Cyber Deception

      

    

      

        
        
How Honeypots Capture and Monitor Cyber Attacks

      

      

        

          
    
            
  1. A honeypot deliberately exposes one or more services that appear to be legitimate targets for cyber attackers.
  2. Once an attacker discovers the exposed service, they interact with it by scanning, authenticating, exploiting vulnerabilities, or executing malicious actions.
  3. Every interaction is monitored and recorded, allowing defenders to analyze the complete attack process without interrupting the attacker.
    4. 
          

        

        
      

      
References: Decoy Environment (D3-DE).

      
    

      

        
        
Choosing the Right Honeypot: Low, Medium, or High Interaction

      


      

        

        
Low-Interaction

        
  • Simulates a basic service (SSH, FTP, HTTP, SMB...).
    • 

  • Safe, easy to deploy, low resource usage.
    • 

  • Limited attacker interaction and intelligence.

      

        
Medium-Interaction

        
  • Emulates a realistic environment, such as a fake Linux shell.
    • 

  • More attacker activity, moderate deployment complexity.
    • 

  • Can be fingerprinted and provides limited realism.

      

        
High-Interaction

        
  • Exposes a real vulnerable operating system or application.
    • 

  • Maximum visibility into attacker behavior and TTPs.
    • 

  • Higher operational risk and infrastructure requirements.

      

      


      
References: A Review of Honeypots: Fingerprinting Techniques, Detection, and Evasion Mechanisms.

    

      

        
        
How Honeytokens Detect Unauthorized Access

      

      

        
        

          
Honeytokens' primary purpose is to detect when a fake asset has been accessed or used, while recording contextual information about the access. In some cases, they can capture details such as the source IP address, application, protocol, or usage context.
    
            
  • Network: URLs and subdomains used as honeytokens to detect the discovery phase. Tip: hidden URLs can be used to identify crawlers.
  • Credentials: API keys, usernames/passwords, certificates, or SSH keys used to detect unauthorized usage. Tip: place them in scripts, configuration files, or repositories where attackers may discover them.
  • Files: File access monitoring can detect any access attempt, while file opening detection can target specific formats such as PDF, DOCX, XLSX, LNK or EXE.
  • Identifiers: Email addresses, credit card payment information, or other unique identifiers used to detect misuse or data leakage.
    • 
          

        

      

      
References: Canarytokens.

Popular Medium-Interaction Honeypot Tools for Cyber Threat Detection

      

      

        

          
Medium-interaction honeypots provide a good balance between deployment simplicity, security, and the amount of attacker activity that can be observed.
    
            
  • Cowrie: SSH and Telnet honeypot. Captures login attempts, commands, and downloaded malware.
  • Dionaea: Malware collection honeypot. Designed to capture malicious payloads and samples.
  • OpenCanary: Lightweight multi-protocol honeypot. Supports services such as SSH, HTTP, SMB, and databases.
  • Honeytrap: Generic network service honeypot for detecting and analyzing attacks.
  • T-Pot: All-in-one honeypot platform integrating multiple tools: Cowrie, Dionaea, Elastic, Kibana, Suricata, SpiderFoot, and more.
    • 
          

        

        
      

      
References: Cowrie Documentation.

Modern Honeypots for Emerging Cyber Threats and New Attack Surfaces

      

    

      

        
        
Honeynets: Observing Modern Multi-Stage Attacks

      

      

        

          
Early malware often relied on automatic propagation mechanisms such as viruses and worms to infect other systems.
Modern attackers increasingly perform targeted operations after the initial compromise, including reconnaissance, privilege escalation, and lateral movement across the environment.
A honeynet connects multiple honeypots to simulate a larger environment and observe these multi-stage attacks with more realistic attack paths.

        

        
      

      
References: The honeynet project..

      
    

      

        
        
Modern Honeypots for Cloud, Containers, and IoT Environments

      

      

        
        

          
Modern infrastructures have expanded beyond traditional servers to include cloud platforms, containers, industrial systems, and billions of connected IoT devices.
Unlike enterprise servers, which are now heavily monitored and protected, many IoT devices remain poorly secured, making them attractive targets for automated attacks that can spread rapidly and compromise large numbers of devices.
To observe these evolving threats, specialized honeypots have been developed for Kubernetes, Docker, cloud platforms (AWS, Azure, GCP), IoT devices, and industrial control systems (ICS/SCADA).

        

      

      
References: IoTPOT: A Novel Honeypot for Revealing Current IoT Threats.

      
    

      

        
        
AI-Powered Analysis for Honeypot Threat Intelligence

      

      

        

          
Modern honeypots can generate thousands of events every day, making manual analysis time-consuming and expensive.
Artificial Intelligence can automatically classify attacks, identify malware families, summarize attacker behaviors, and correlate similar incidents across multiple honeypots.
By accelerating threat analysis, AI helps security teams reduce investigation time, improve response efficiency, and lower operational costs.

        

        
      

      
References: Honeypot's Best Friend ? Investigating ChatGPT's Ability to Evaluate Honeypot Logs.

      
    

      

        
        
Could Honeypots Help Analyze AI-Driven Cyber Attacks ?

      

      

        
        

          
AI-assisted attacks are becoming increasingly common, and fully autonomous attack campaigns may become an emerging threat for organizations with limited security capabilities.
Unlike human attackers, AI agents may execute predefined attack workflows without questioning the authenticity of the target unless explicitly instructed to detect deception.
If AI-driven attacks become widespread, honeypots could provide a scalable way to observe their behaviors, identify common attack patterns, and adapt defensive strategies.

        

      

      
References: Disrupting an AI-enabled cyber espionage campaign.

Balancing Honeypot Security and Realism

      

      

        

          
Security recommendations reduce the risk of operating a honeypot, but they often decrease its realism and the attacker's freedom of action.
    
            
  • Isolate the honeypot to prevent access to production systems.
  • Restrict outbound connections to avoid malware propagation or the loss of valuable IOCs.
  • Monitor logs continuously to detect unexpected activity and investigate attacks.
  • Keep the environment up to date to reduce the risk of unintended compromise.
  • Use a dedicated infrastructure and never connect a honeypot directly to critical systems.
    • 
          

        

        
      

      
References: Bitter Harvest: Systematically Fingerprinting Low and Medium-interaction Honeypots at Internet Scale.

      
    

      

        
      

      

        
Real-World Honeypot Results, Threat Intelligence, and Lessons Learned

      

    

      

        
        
What Do Medium-Interaction Honeypots Capture in Practice ?

      

      

        

          
Medium-interaction honeypots primarily collect well-known automated attacks rather than previously unseen threats.
Typical observations include SSH brute-force attacks (especially against the root account), Nmap and Masscan scans, Mirai, Gafgyt, and XMRig malware, IoT attacks, SMB probing, and the exploitation of recently disclosed vulnerabilities.
A typical Internet-exposed SSH honeypot can record more than 20,000 connection attempts from around 900 IP addresses across 45 countries in just 24 hours, while collecting hundreds of malware samples.

        

        
      

      
References: Expert System Assessing Threat Level of Attacks on a Hybrid SSH Honeynet.

      
    

      

        
        
What Human Attackers Really Do on High-Interaction Honeypots

      

      

        
        

          
Unlike medium-interaction honeypots, high-interaction honeypots can reveal real attacker behavior after the initial compromise.
Common observations include changing passwords, downloading tools (mostly with wget, often abandoning the attempt if it fails), storing files in /tmp, /var/tmp, or /dev/shm, performing internal network reconnaissance, escalating privileges with rootkits, sending phishing emails, and cleaning traces of the intrusion (~30% of observed cases).
Although the environment allowed lateral movement, only one attempt was observed during six months of monitoring. This likely reflects attacker objectives, early detection of the honeypot, or environmental constraints rather than the absence of lateral movement in real-world intrusions.

        

      

      
References: Lessons Learned from the Deployment of a High-Interaction Honeypot.

      
    

      

        
        
How Honeypots Support Cyber Threat Intelligence and Campaign Tracking

      

      

        

          
While many honeypots capture known automated attacks, strategically deployed honeypots can help security researchers document new campaigns, malware variants, and attacker infrastructure.
Examples include the discovery of a new TeamTNT botnet variant targeting Docker environments and the analysis of attackers exploiting known vulnerabilities such as Drupal (CVE-2018-7600), Oracle WebLogic (CVE-2017-10271), and Spring Data Commons (CVE-2018-1273).
In some cases, small artifacts left by attackers can support attribution efforts. A specific string (qq952135763) found during analysis was linked to online accounts, repositories, and personal websites, helping researchers identify a potential Chinese-speaking threat actor.

        

        
      

      
References: Unit 42 – Docker Honeypot Reveals New TeamTNT Activity, Cisco Talos – Honeypot Analysis Reveals Chinese Threat Actor Activity.

      
    

      

        
        
Benefits and Value of Well-Designed Honeypots and Honeytokens

      

      

        
        

          
The value of honeypots and honeytokens strongly depends on their quality, placement, and operational management.
Honeytokens:
    
            
  • Highly effective detection mechanism with a low false-positive rate.
  • Low deployment cost and minimal operational overhead.
    • 
          
Honeypots:
    
            
  • Provide low-cost visibility into attacker activity and commonly used tools and techniques.
  • High-quality honeypots can generate valuable intelligence by capturing advanced attacker behaviors and campaigns.
  • However, they require time, infrastructure, and the right exposure to attract relevant threats.
    • 
          

        

      

      
References: Proactive Detection of Security Incidents II: Honeypots.

      
    

      

        
        
Limitations of Honeypots

      

      

        

          
    
            
  • Depend on attacker interaction: No value if attackers do not discover or engage with the honeypot.
  • Risk of detection: Skilled attackers may identify honeypots through behavioral or environmental fingerprints.
  • Potential security risk: A compromised honeypot can be abused as a pivot point or attack infrastructure.
    • 
          

        

        
      

      
References: Honeypots – Computer and Information Security Handbook.

Conclusion: Honeypots Turning Attacks into Cybersecurity Intelligence

      

    

      

        
        
Key Takeaways: The Role and Limits of Honeypots

      

      

        

          
A honeypot does not block attackers, it attracts them to observe their actions and collect valuable intelligence.
It provides visibility into threats, attacker techniques, tools, and behaviors that would otherwise remain unknown.
Its effectiveness depends on the quality of the deployment, the realism of the environment, and proper monitoring.
Honeypots are valuable tools for threat detection, cybersecurity research, and training, but they must be deployed carefully because a compromised honeypot can create security risks.
